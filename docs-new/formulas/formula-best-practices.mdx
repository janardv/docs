Good formulas are not only correct. They are also easy for another person to read, review, and safely change. A formula section that only one model builder understands becomes a risk as the model grows.

## Write one business rule at a time

Keep each formula focused on one clear job when possible.

If a variable needs to handle seasonality, cutoff behavior, overrides, and allocation logic, split that work into helper variables before you write the final output formula.

## Standardize names and core terms

Use stable names so formulas read like plain language.

Use the same shared terms across the team:

- `Last actual date` for the actuals cutoff
- `actual formula` for historical logic
- `planned formula` for forward-looking logic
- `relative time` for moving time references

Consistent wording reduces mistakes during reviews and handoffs.

## Separate actual and planned logic intentionally

If historical and forward-looking behavior differ, make that split obvious.

Do not hide the cutoff inside a hard-coded date or an unclear nested expression. Tie the rule directly to the [`Last actual date`](/docs-new/time/last-actual-date) or to clearly named helper variables.

## Prefer helper variables over deep nesting

Use [Helper variables and modifiers](/docs-new/formulas/helper-variables-and-modifiers) when:

- the same condition appears more than once
- a formula needs several branches
- the final output should summarize logic rather than contain every detail
- a reviewer needs business labels, not raw implementation steps

Shorter formulas are easier to audit and safer to change.

## Validate at detail and summary level

Do not stop after one cell looks correct.

Review formulas at:

- the source variable
- the relevant dimension detail
- the first period before and after the cutoff
- quarter and year summaries where applicable

That is often where aggregation or context issues become visible.

## Avoid hard-coded time logic

Use [`Relative time`](/docs-new/time/relative-time) and built-in time helpers when the business rule should continue to work after the model moves forward.

Hard-coded periods create maintenance work and make close cycles riskier.

## Before you publish a model

Use this checklist before you hand the model to other users:

- confirm the key formulas still match the business rule
- confirm the `actual formula` and `planned formula` behave correctly around the cutoff
- confirm quarter and year summaries still make sense
- confirm important formulas use readable helper variables where needed
- confirm unusual logic is easy to explain to another planner
